# **Source Map and Data Architecture Report for GCC Compass**

## **1\. Executive Summary**

The Indian Global Capability Centre (GCC) ecosystem has undergone a profound structural evolution, maturing from a traditional cost-arbitrage offshore destination into the world’s preeminent engine for digital innovation and enterprise transformation. The landscape currently comprises an estimated 2,117 operational GCC units, employing nearly 1.9 million professionals and generating annual revenues approaching \$64 billion to \$100 billion1. Capturing approximately 53% of the global GCC footprint4, these centres are increasingly pivoting toward high-value mandates, particularly in artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), cybersecurity, and core product engineering5. This paradigm shift has given rise to the "AI-native GCC," fundamentally altering talent acquisition strategies, location benchmarking, and operational cost models across the subcontinent.  
Despite India's undisputed dominance in this sector, strategic intelligence regarding the ecosystem remains highly fragmented. Foreign enterprises evaluating setup and expansion strategies face an opaque data environment, forced to manually synthesize insights across disparate real estate market reports, shifting state government policies, complex compliance filings, and specialized consulting indices. This informational asymmetry introduces significant friction into the decision-making process, often leading to sub-optimal location choices, miscalculated talent budgets, and regulatory blind spots.  
The "GCC Compass" product is conceptualized to eradicate this friction by centralizing this fragmented intelligence into a unified, data-driven platform. Serving as a comprehensive prototype for foreign entities, GCC Compass will deliver interactive location benchmarking, precise capability mapping, and dynamic operational modeling. This report delivers an exhaustive source map and architectural blueprint for the platform. It critically evaluates primary and secondary data repositories, outlines a strategic Minimum Viable Product (MVP) dataset, constructs highly relational data schemas, and assesses the technical feasibility of automated data extraction via web crawling. Furthermore, the analysis synthesizes critical second- and third-order insights concerning bifurcated talent premiums, real estate dual-use dynamics, revised transfer pricing safe harbours, and incorporation frameworks. These foundational elements ensure that the GCC Compass AI-powered analyst and benchmarking tools are grounded in the most authoritative, granular, and up-to-date information available in the market.

## **3\. Recommended MVP Dataset Plan**

To launch a highly functional prototype of GCC Compass that immediately demonstrates value to foreign enterprise users, the initial dataset should be meticulously curated rather than bulk-scraped. This approach ensures high data fidelity and effectively demonstrates the complex interconnectedness of the platform's modules. The MVP dataset must establish the foundational logic linking corporate entities, geographical locations, regulatory policies, and financial models.  
The execution strategy for the MVP requires a phased rollout across the five core data domains. For the foundational GCC Atlas, the objective is to curate 40 to 80 records representing a cross-section of the market. This seed data should be extracted by targeting the top global brands operating in India from the Flexiple GCC Directory, prioritizing recognizable names across sectors such as Accenture, Amazon, JPMorgan, Microsoft, Target, and Wells Fargo8. Once identified, these entities require deep data enrichment. Analysts must manually search the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) records to ascertain their registered Indian entity names, recognizing that a parent company like "Microsoft" operates through specific subsidiaries like "Microsoft India (R\&D) Private Limited"9. These legal entities must then be mapped to specific SEZ or STPI notifications by parsing the SEZ India Board of Approval minutes13. Finally, each record should be tagged by maturity stage—classifying them as a Cost Centre, Centre of Excellence (CoE), or Transformation Hub—based on qualitative assessments derived from published Nasscom and Zinnov case studies37.  
Simultaneously, the City Benchmarking module requires the assembly of 8 to 10 comprehensive city profiles. The target list must include established Tier-1 metros such as Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Delhi NCR (Gurugram/Noida), and Mumbai, alongside emerging specialized hubs like Ahmedabad (GIFT City) and critical Tier-2 frontiers like Coimbatore or Kochi4. Data assembly for this module involves utilizing CBRE and JLL market reports to extract Grade A commercial rents and vacancy rates24. This real estate data must be synthesized with talent depth indicators and regional attrition metrics from Zinnov's city-tier analyses, explicitly highlighting that Tier-2 office rentals are 40–50% lower than Tier-1 rates, while talent costs remain 20–30% lower4.  
To populate the State Policy layer, 7 to 10 distinct records must be created by downloading official PDF policy documents. Crucial targets include Karnataka’s IT Policy 2025-3017, Maharashtra’s IT/ITeS Policy 202319, Uttar Pradesh’s GCC Policy 202421, and Odisha’s Data Centre Policy 202220. The extraction process must focus on digitizing specific numeric incentives, translating narrative policy into structured calculator variables—such as UP's payroll subsidy of up to Rs 20 crore annually or Odisha's 100% stamp duty exemption20.  
Finally, the Build vs Buy Calculator and Stakeholder Layer require precise cost and compliance inputs. The calculator must hardcode Zinnov’s 2026 talent benchmarking data, setting baseline salary inflation at 9.8%, while applying algorithmic modifiers for AI/ML roles indexed at a 21.1% premium15. Tax compliance logic must incorporate the revised Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) Safe Harbour margin of 15.5% for IT/ITeS services28. Concurrently, the stakeholder database should be seeded with 20 to 30 initial profiles representing Big 4 advisory practices, premier legal firms, and leading EOR providers, utilizing their publicly available GCC setup playbooks to outline services offered27.

## **4\. Detailed Source Notes by Module**

The architecture of GCC Compass demands a nuanced understanding of where data originates, how it is structured, and the inherent limitations of each source. The following sections detail the data acquisition strategy for each core module.

### **4.1. GCC Atlas Module**

The GCC Atlas serves as the central nervous system of the platform, requiring granular, company-level intelligence. The Indian landscape is remarkably diverse, comprising operational footprints that range from massive 10,000+ employee delivery campuses to highly specialized, boutique AI laboratories.  
The primary discovery engine for this module is the Flexiple GCC Directory (flexiple.com/gcc/india). This source provides a filterable repository of 536 verified GCCs, while actively tracking up to 2,117 macro-level units across the subcontinent. It is invaluable for extracting parent headquarters locations, founding years, key operational roles (e.g., Software Engineer, Data Analyst), and estimated headcount bands8. While open access, the data relies on aggregated professional network analytics; consequently, real-time accuracy regarding headcount fluctuations may lag, and there is a risk of misclassifying IT services contractors as direct GCC employees.  
To establish legal and structural veracity, the platform must rely on the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) database (mca.gov.in). This portal provides authoritative incorporation records, SPICe+ (INC-32) filings, master data, paid-up capital, and director registries9. However, it is structurally hostile to automated extraction, protected by CAPTCHAs and strict access controls. Furthermore, foreign enterprises frequently incorporate under abstract nomenclatures, necessitating human analysts to map global brand names to their specific Indian subsidiaries.  
Geospatial mapping and facility tracking require data from the SEZ India Approvals portal (sezindia.gov.in). By analyzing Board of Approval (BoA) minutes and lists of formally approved Special Economic Zones, GCC Compass can link corporate entities to specific tech parks10. The primary limitation here is technical; the data is entombed within unstructured PDF meeting minutes, requiring sophisticated parsing to extract tenant names, expansion requests, and critical Rule 11A (dual-use) commercial demarcations12.

### **4.2. City Benchmarking Module**

The City Benchmarking tool must synthesize complex variables to help enterprises find the optimal equilibrium between operational cost, talent depth, and infrastructure resilience.  
Real estate cost modeling relies heavily on CBRE and JLL India Office Market Reports. These sources provide authoritative metrics on gross absorption rates, Grade A stock availability, and micro-market commercial rents, tracking significant milestones such as the record absorption achieved in the first three quarters of 202524. While highly reliable, these reports are systematically biased toward the top seven metropolitan areas. Acquiring granular data for emerging Tier-2 cities like Jaipur, Indore, or Visakhapatnam requires synthesizing alternative, localized sources.  
To evaluate talent depth and the strategic viability of emerging hubs, Nasscom and Zinnov ecosystem reports are indispensable. These sources quantify the exact advantages of the Tier-2 frontier, explicitly detailing that office rentals in these markets operate at 40–50% of Tier-1 rates, while experienced GCC professional salaries reflect a 20–30% discount4. Critically, these reports reveal that employee attrition in Tier-2 cities is structurally lower, hovering at 12–15% compared to the volatile 20–25% seen in major metros4. Integrating this intelligence requires translating qualitative strategic context into quantitative scoring metrics for the benchmarking algorithm.

### **4.3. Build vs Buy Calculator Module**

This module operates as the commercial modeling engine of GCC Compass, empowering CFOs and strategy leaders to quantitatively evaluate the ROI of Employer of Record (EOR), Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT), and Direct Entity setup pathways.  
Tax and compliance assumptions are heavily informed by Big 4 advisory insights (PwC, EY) and CBDT notifications. A critical variable for the calculator's operational expenditure logic is the Transfer Pricing Safe Harbour Rules. Following the Union Budget 2026, the government consolidated software development, IT-enabled services (ITeS), KPO, and Contract R\&D into a single category, applying a uniform 15.5% margin threshold on operating expenses for international transactions up to INR 2,000 crore28. This metric is vital for accurately projecting the true cost of operating a captive center.  
For evaluating the EOR pathway, data from providers like Deel and Flexiple offers baseline assumptions regarding setup timelines, statutory benefits (such as the 12% EPF contribution), and professional taxes30. While these platforms claim rapid deployment timelines—often citing 3 days for EOR setups compared to 3-6 months for direct entities—this data inherently promotes their proprietary solutions. The calculator must balance these claims by modeling the long-term margin erosion caused by EOR markups at scale, utilizing industry benchmarks which indicate that EOR models typically lose financial viability once a team exceeds 30 to 50 employees.  
Modeling the Direct Entity route requires strict adherence to incorporation guidance provided by Invest India and the MCA. The calculator must reflect Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) guidelines permitting 100% foreign equity via the automatic route for IT/ITeS, alongside FEMA regulations governing the capitalization of Wholly Owned Subsidiaries (WOS)9.

### **4.4. Talent and Compensation Data Module**

Given that talent acquisition and retention account for 60% to 70% of a GCC's operational expenditure, precise compensation data is critical to the platform's utility.  
The primary intelligence for this module stems from Zinnov and Nasscom salary benchmarking reports for 2025 and 2026\. A superficial analysis might suggest the talent market is stabilizing, with average salary hikes across Indian GCCs settling at 9.8% in 202615. However, integrating this flat rate into the calculator would be fundamentally flawed. The data reveals a highly bifurcated market driven by a severe demand-supply gap; while the overall pool of AI professionals reached 416,000, demand outstrips supply by 51%16. Consequently, AI and ML engineering roles demand a 21.1% salary increase, cybersecurity roles require 20.0%, and high performers generally receive 1.8 times the average hike15.  
Furthermore, GCC Compass must accurately model the "within-market premium stack." The data explicitly demonstrates that standard GCC roles pay a 12–20% premium over domestic IT services baselines, with AI/ML capabilities demanding an additional 30–40% premium on top of that elevated base45. The calculator must dynamically apply these scarcity multipliers based on the user's specific technology stack inputs to prevent catastrophic budget underestimation in the critical third year of operations.

### **4.6. Stakeholder Layer Module**

The Stakeholder Layer transforms GCC Compass from a static intelligence tool into a dynamic marketplace, connecting foreign enterprises with verified domestic service providers.  
Initial data seeding will rely on publicly available service portfolios and playbooks from major consulting and real estate firms (e.g., EY, PwC, Zinnov, CBRE). These firms have heavily productized their offerings, such as EY and Zinnov's "GCC-as-a-Service" models, and PwC's frameworks for establishing Digital GCC Centres of Excellence27. The strategic imperative here is generating highly qualified demand signals. A verified stakeholder will derive immense value from a platform that can deliver precise procurement intents—for example, signaling that a U.S.-based Series C SaaS firm is actively evaluating a 50-person BOT setup in Hyderabad within the next six months.

## **5\. Crawler Recommendation**

Automated data extraction is highly viable for maintaining the currency of specific modules, but it poses significant legal and technical risks if misapplied. A bifurcated strategy utilizing Tinyfish or similar headless scraping architectures is recommended.  
Tinyfish is strongly recommended for continuous monitoring of state investment portals (e.g., investindia.gov.in, state department of industry domains) to detect policy amendments, draft notifications, and relevant press releases. Furthermore, the crawler should be deployed against the SEZ India portal to automatically detect and download newly uploaded Board of Approval (BoA) PDF minutes, passing them to an OCR pipeline for subsequent entity extraction11.  
Conversely, automated scraping must be strictly prohibited against the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) portal (mca.gov.in). The MCA employs robust anti-bot architectures, including dynamic session tokens and advanced CAPTCHAs, and aggressively enforces Terms of Service that prohibit automated extraction. Attempting to crawl this infrastructure risks immediate IP bans and potential legal repercussions. Similarly, deploying deep crawlers against gated compensation platforms or proprietary databases risks intellectual property infringement.  
For directory aggregators like Flexiple, scraping can be utilized to gather the initial MVP seed data. However, ethical scraping protocols—strictly respecting robots.txt directives and applying conservative rate limits—must be enforced. The long-term product strategy must pivot the Stakeholder Layer away from scraped intelligence, transitioning GCC Compass into a self-reported platform where service providers claim profiles, maintain their own data, and verify their credentials to access the marketplace.

## **7\. Confidence Scoring System**

To ensure enterprise users explicitly trust the data driving their multi-million dollar investment decisions, a 0-100 Confidence Score must be dynamically calculated for every GCC record. This algorithmic rating evaluates the provenance, recency, and corroboration of the underlying data points.  
The logic framework begins with a Base Score dictated by the source type. Primary, official government or corporate sources—such as the MCA, SEZ India portals, or direct company press releases—are awarded an immediate \+60 points. Secondary, professionally vetted sources like Zinnov industry reports or Big 4 analyses receive \+40 points. Tertiary, crowdsourced, or unverified data scraped from job boards garners only \+20 points.  
This base is then modified by a Recency Multiplier to account for data decay. Information updated within the last 6 months retains a 1.0x multiplier. Data aged 6 to 12 months is reduced to 0.8x, 1 to 2 years drops to 0.5x, and any data older than 2 years is severely penalized with a 0.2x multiplier.  
Finally, the algorithm applies additive bonuses. A Cross-Verification Bonus of \+20 points is awarded if a critical data point (such as headcount) is independently corroborated by two or more distinct source categories (e.g., a Flexiple listing matching an SEZ BoA minute). A Data Completeness Bonus of \+10 points is applied if all core fields (Parent HQ, Indian Entity, City, Function, Headcount) are fully populated.  
For example, a GCC record scraped exclusively from a job board two years ago would generate a base score of 20, multiplied by 0.2 for recency, yielding a deeply untrustworthy score of 4\. Conversely, an entity verified via the MCA and corroborated by a recent EY market report would score (60 \* 1.0) \+ (40 \* 1.0) \+ 20 (cross-verification) \= 120, which is algorithmically capped at 100, designating it as "High Confidence."

## **8\. Red Flags / Limitations**

The architecture and deployment of GCC Compass involve several systemic, regulatory, and technical risks that must be proactively mitigated within the product design.  
A primary concern is the risk of hallucination within the AI Analyst module. When users query complex compliance frameworks, Large Language Models (LLMs) are prone to generating generalized global tax advice rather than specific, localized mandates. For instance, an ungrounded model might fail to specify the nuanced Indian CBDT Safe Harbour rules28. To mitigate this, the system must implement stringent Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) guardrails. The AI Analyst must be architecturally restricted to citing only the verified text embedded within the source\_citation table. If the database lacks specific information to answer a query, the system must be programmed to unequivocally state: "Insufficient verified data in the GCC Compass database."  
Real estate tracking presents another significant layer of complexity due to Rule 11A of the SEZ Rules, 2006\. This legislation permits the formal demarcation of non-processing areas within Special Economic Zones for "dual use," effectively allowing developers to lease commercial office space to non-SEZ entities12. Consequently, an IT park bearing an SEZ designation does not guarantee that a specific tenant within that park is operating as an export-oriented unit or receiving SEZ tax benefits. The City Benchmarking module must be designed to clearly differentiate between pure SEZ processing zones, Software Technology Parks of India (STPI) units, and standard Domestic Tariff Area (DTA) commercial stock to prevent users from making flawed tax assumptions based purely on location.  
Furthermore, the extreme volatility in salary data introduces severe risks to the Build vs Buy Calculator. Providing users with a generalized, flat "Software Engineer" salary band is analytically dangerous. The underlying data indicates severe stratification in talent costs across the ecosystem. While base salary hikes average 9.8%, the chronic 51% supply shortfall for AI professionals has driven AI/ML salary increases to 21.1%, with cybersecurity following closely at 20.0%15. More critically, the market operates on a compounding premium stack: GCCs inherently pay a 12-20% baseline premium over domestic IT services firms, and highly sought-after AI/ML capabilities command an additional 30-40% premium on top of that elevated baseline45. If the calculator relies on blended averages, a CFO planning a specialized AI Centre of Excellence will face catastrophic budget blowouts by the third year of operation. The tool must mandate that users input their specific technology stack and dynamically apply these scarcity multipliers to generate accurate projections.  
Finally, the platform must navigate the friction inherent in transfer pricing and tax modeling. While incorporating the revised 15.5% Safe Harbour margin for IT services28 is essential for modeling operational expenditures, the calculator must avoid presenting this as an absolute guarantee. Transfer pricing disputes are a historical reality for foreign entities scaling operations in India50. The product interface must include persistent disclaimers indicating that actual, binding margins require Advance Pricing Agreements (APAs) negotiated by authorized tax counsel, reinforcing that GCC Compass is a strategic modeling tool rather than a substitute for formal legal and financial advisory.
